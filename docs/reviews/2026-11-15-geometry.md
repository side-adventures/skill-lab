## Part 1: Test audit (test-auditor)

**Overall 3 / 5, close to 4.** Judged from code and SVG only (no browser); a throwaway simulation (4000 rounds per task) checked naive guesses, deleted afterwards.

**What it does now:** 8 tasks per run, 2 picked at random from each of 4 groups, all in random order:
- **Centres:** Center of the arc, Center of the dots, Balance point.
- **Constructions:** Where the lines cross, Complete the square, Mirror point.
- **Angles:** Make X°, Make X % of the angle, Make it parallel.
- **Lengths & areas:** Copy the length, Middle of the line (zigzag), Match the size.

Click or drag with no time limit, then Done (Enter / Space also work). Each task scores 0–100 %, falling in a straight line to 0 at its own limit. Afterwards the exact solution (dashed green) and the "0 % zone" (faint green hairlines) are shown. Score = average of the 8 tasks; the result shows best task, weakest task and one chip per task.

### 1. Name
- **Current: Eye for Geometry.** Still accurate; "geometry" covers the broader test (areas, reflection, extrapolation).
- **Alternatives:** "Eyeballing" (the known game with the same idea), "Geometry by Eye", "Sharp Eye".
- **Recommendation:** keep **Eye for Geometry**.

**Task titles (the user's names kept; only real problems flagged):**

|---|---|---|

Balance point, in the same group, *is* the average position, so the two tasks pull against each other. A player who places the average position scores **about 54 %** on average (simulation), purely from how the title reads. | Yes, once understood. |
| Balance point | Yes. "Balance on a pin" is a nice physical cue. | Yes. |

| Complete the square | Yes. | Yes. |
| **Mirror point** | Yes. Long, but "straight across the line" removes the ambiguity. | Yes. The limit scales with the dot's distance from the line, which is right. |
| Make X° | Yes. | Partly. Needs a learned sense of degrees, not only perception. Too easy at ±25° (about 95 % in the user's data). |
| **Make X % of the angle** | **Problem.** The prompt reads "Turn the arm to close the angle until it is X % as wide as now." Once the arm moves, "now" no longer means the full angle. | Yes, but the limit is a fixed 15° whatever the full angle is. |
| Make it parallel | Yes. | Yes. |
| Copy the length | Yes. | Yes; also measures a known illusion (below). |

**Suggested text:**
- **Center of the dots** → title **"Equally far from the dots"** (short name "Equal distance" or keep "Dots center"). Alternative: "Center of the dot circle". Keep the prompt.
- **Make X % of the angle** prompt → "Turn the arm until the angle is 40 % as wide as the whole shaded angle." (The full angle is drawn with a light fill.)
- **Where the lines cross** prompt (optional) → "Imagine both lines carry on past their ends. Place the point where they would cross."

### 2. Scientific relevance

**Overall paradigm:** still closest to Matthias Wandel's **Eyeballing Game**, which repeats each of its 7 tasks **3 times** (21 trials) and reports the average error in pixels, or degrees × 2 for angle tasks ([woodgears about page](https://www.woodgears.ca/eyeball/about.html), [kottke](https://also.kottke.org/08/10/the-eyeballing-game)). Drag-to-adjust with no time limit is the classic **method of adjustment**.

**Centres**
- **Balance point:** the perceived centre of flat shapes lies close to the area centroid; errors shrink as shapes have more sides ([ane.pl study](https://ane.pl/index.php/ane/article/download/967/967/989)). Baud-Bovy & Gentaz (2004) found triangle-centre responses near the centroid in children and adults ([core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/224271013.pdf); only the snippet was readable). Proffitt et al. (1983): the perceived centre depends mainly on the outline ([record](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/dennis-proffitt/publications/roles-contour-and-luminance-distribution-determining-perceived-centers)). Well grounded.
- **Center of the arc:** the arc-size illusion makes short arcs look flatter, pushing the guessed centre too far out. Kang et al. (2016): perceived curvature rises with arc length only up to about 60° ([Plymouth record](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/10189); Tolansky 1964 cited there). With 90–180° arcs the bias should be small. Good choice.
- **Center of the dots:** no direct research found. It's a geometric construction (the centre of the circle through the dots) more than a perception judgment, and structurally the **same answer type as Center of the arc**. One run in three gets both.

**Constructions**
- **Where the lines cross:** extrapolating lines, tied to the Poggendorff literature, where people show systematic extrapolation errors even without the classic inducing lines (Melmoth et al. 2009, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2852533)). Uncertainty grows roughly with 1 / sin(crossing angle): a 30° crossing is clearly harder than an 80° one, but the 0 % ring (50 % of the gap) ignores the angle.
- **Mirror point:** symmetry is most salient around a vertical axis, then horizontal; slanted axes are harder (Wenderoth 1994, *Perception* 23:221, [record](https://scilit.com/publications/275fe249dd5ebffd954802e1443e9846)). The test always uses a 15–75° slant: deliberately the harder range, consistent across runs.
- **Complete the square:** the Eyeballing "parallelogram / right angle" task. Random rotation brings in the **oblique effect** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_effect)).

**Angles**
- **Make X°:** when adults draw angles, errors pull toward 90° (small angles drawn too large, large ones too small); perception and drawing biases correlate (Ostrofsky et al. 2015, [PDF](https://stockton.edu/social-behavioral-sciences/documents/ostrofsky2015angle.pdf); [Iowa State thesis](https://lib.dr.iastate.edu/etd/13750/)).
- **X % of the angle:** a proportion judgment, as in pie charts: small proportions overestimated, large ones underestimated (Spence's power model; Hollands & Dyre 2000; [summary in Patalano et al. 2023](https://apatalano.faculty.wesleyan.edu/files/2023/02/Patalano-et-al.-2023.pdf)). Cleveland & McGill rank angle judgments below position and length ([course summary](https://info3312.infosci.cornell.edu/ae/graphical-perception.html)). A good addition, more perceptual than "Name the angle" was.
- **Make it parallel:** orientation matching between separated lines (oblique effect applies). No source with numbers found; the auditor's estimate: orientation thresholds are a few degrees, so ±10° is generous.

**Lengths & areas**
- **Copy the length:** length matching by adjustment; Weber fractions from about 1 % to 28 % depending on the procedure ([Hamburger & Hansen 2010](https://www.allpsych.uni-giessen.de/hansen/publications/HamburgerHansen2010AttenPerceptPsychophys.pdf)).
  - **New point:** the upper line is 35–145° (often steep), your line roughly flat (−15 to 20°). That's the setup of the **vertical–horizontal illusion**: steep lines look about 5–10 % longer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical%E2%80%93horizontal_illusion)). Expect players to make the flat line systematically **too long**; a 7 % bias alone costs about 28 points with the ±25 % limit. Fine and even a nice fact to show, but the task partly measures how strongly you're fooled by the illusion.
- **Middle of the line (zigzag):** not classic line bisection (tiny bias in healthy adults; pseudoneglect, Jewell & McCourt 2000) but closer to **path-length estimation**: slightly zigzag paths are overestimated, strongly zigzag ones underestimated (Chen, Schütt & Ma, VSS poster, [abstract](https://www.visionsciences.org/presentation/?id=3341)). Explains why the user's data shows it hard and swingy (~65 %).
- **Match the size:** area judgments follow a power law with exponent about 0.8; people tend to judge one dimension and square it (Teghtsoonian 1965, via the [Macmillan et al. table](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03203953)). Comparing different shapes is biased by whichever dimension stands out (Krider, Raghubir & Krishna 2001, *Marketing Science* 20:405, [record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v20y2001i4p405-425.html)). So difficulty depends on the pair (12 ordered pairs; the star is concave) — likely the main reason it scores ~51 % even with its wide limit. Simple tricks score low (simulation): same outer radius ~30 %, same width ~23 %.

**Do the 4 groups make sense?** Yes, as content groups, and they keep every run's mix the same, which limits luck. Not separate abilities in the research sense. Three small issues: centres has two "centre of a circle" tasks (Arc, Dots); Center of the dots is as much a construction as Mirror point; angles mixes one degree-knowledge task (Make X°) with two perceptual ones. None needs changing; the smallest tweak would be never putting Arc and Dots in the same run.

**How accurate is the score?**
- **Meaning: better than before.** The typed "Name the angle" and the duplicate split task are gone; the new tasks each map onto a known perception finding.
- **Precision: about the same, possibly a bit lower.** 8 single trials instead of 10 (research ~20 adjustments per condition; Eyeballing Game 3 per kind). **New luck: which mix you get** (81 possible mixes); swapping one task can move the score by about 3–5 points. A `scoring-reviewer` run on spread with the 2-per-group mix is recommended. Remaining per-round luck: crossing angle (Where the lines cross), shape pair (Match the size), size of the full angle (X % of the angle).
- **Ceiling / floor:** no practical ceiling; floor unlikely. Wild guesses mostly score near 0 (board centre: Square 0 %, Crossing 2 %, Mirror 3 %) and 20–24 % on Arc, Balance and Zigzag.
- **Practice effects:** stronger now (solution and 0 % zone shown every task). Fine, but scores will rise over the first runs.

**What it says about you:** how precisely you place centres, extend lines, mirror points and judge angles, lengths and areas by eye — useful for drawing, DIY, layout and design. Not maths or IQ.

**Fair limits, the research view (information only):**
- A common rule: 0 % limit at about **3–4× a typical adult error**, so a typical player lands around 70–75 %.
- Tie each limit to what drives the error: lengths in % of the length (Weber); point tasks in % of the judged distance (already done); proportions in % of the reference, not absolute degrees.
- **X % of the angle:** 15° is 9 % of a 160° angle but 30 % of a 50° one; a limit of about **15 % of the full angle** would be fairer (score change).
- **Make X°:** ±25° is at the ceiling; given drawing biases of a few to ~10° toward 90°, about **±12–15°**.
- **Where the lines cross:** scale the ring with 1 / sin(crossing angle), or narrow the range (e.g. 45–80°).
- **Copy the length:** ±25 % is reasonable to generous.
- **Match the size:** no cross-shape norms found; keep the wide limit, or drop hard pairs (e.g. star vs triangle) if per-pair spread shows in the data.
- No numeric norms exist for these tasks as set up here; collecting own data is the right plan.

**What's missing (no score change):** per-task averages across your history (already saved under fixed short names) — far more reliable than one run's "Weakest task"; a tendency line where one exists ("too long" on Copy the length, "too small / too big" on Match the size).

## Part 2: Scoring review (scoring-reviewer)

**Short answer:** no loophole. No shortcut gets close to an honest weak player. Generation always ends and stays on the board. What decides a single run is per-task noise, more than tolerance imbalance or which tasks get drawn. Two small generation problems and one confusing 0 % line.

Numbers from `src/tests/geometry/scoring.review.test.ts` (run with `npx vitest run src/tests/geometry --silent=false`). Two tests that prove Problems 1 and 2 are marked `it.fails` (they pass while the issue exists, and turn red once it's fixed).

**Player model:** bell-curve errors: positions off by a % of the judged distance, angles by degrees, lengths and areas by %; 5 % of tasks sloppy (3× noise).
- **Model A:** the same noise everywhere (position 8 %, angle 4°, length 8 %).
- **Model B:** each task's noise tuned so the average player matches the user's per-task averages.
- Weak / strong players: 1.6× / 0.6× the average player's noise.

### Problems, by severity

**1. Match the size: in about 3 % of these tasks, clicking Done straight away scores over 50 %** (medium-low).
- When the goal is large (≥ 15.6) and the start is drawn on the "too big" side, the start is capped at the handle limit 25, which can be almost exactly the goal. Happens in 13 % of Size tasks; those starts score 29 % on average, 10 % of them over 80 %.
- Over all Size tasks the start scores 4.4 % on average, above 0 in 18 %, above 50 % in 3 %.
- In 18 % of Size tasks the "too big" 0 % outline is past what the handle can reach (largest goal 24.8).
- **Suggested fix:** if goal × 1.3 > 25, start on the "too small" side; keep goals ≤ ~19 (25 / 1.3) or raise the handle limit. Small score change; no bump in development.

**2. Zigzag: about 2 % of the lines nearly touch themselves** (low-medium).
- The crossing check uses strict sign tests, so sections passing very close are allowed: in 5000 lines, 121 come within 3 units of themselves and 228 within 5; closest 0.02 (looks like a crossing). Near such a spot a click can snap onto the other section.
- Clicking exactly at the middle always snaps correctly (0 failures in 5000).
- **Suggested fix:** redraw when two non-neighbouring sections come closer than ~5 units (layout only).

**3. Make X°: for targets under 25° or over 155°, one drawn 0 % line sits on a near-perfect answer** (low, partly intended).
- Below 0° the angle folds back like past 180°: target 12°, line at −13° makes a 13° angle → 96 %. In 4000 tasks, 362 lines below 0° and 332 past 180° score above 0, up to 96 % (~18 % of Make X° tasks). The spec only mentions past 180°.
- **Suggested fix:** add "or below 0°" to the spec, or better: don't draw a limit on the far side of the fixed line or past 180°.

**4. X % of the angle: luck from how wide the angle is** (information; tolerances stay for now).
- Fixed 15° limit on a 50–160° angle: the same 6 %-of-the-angle error scores 79.3 % on 50–80° angles and 56.5 % on 130–160° ones.
- **Possible fix later:** a limit as a share of the angle, e.g. 12 %.
